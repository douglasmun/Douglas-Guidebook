Is Microsoft’s fix enough?
No.
On 19 November Microsoft closed the path into the management plane of APIM Classic instances. It revoked nothing that was copied through it. Named values, subscription keys, identity-provider client secrets and direct-management tokens taken before then still work for whoever holds them, until each expires or is replaced where it was issued. Norway’s NSM tells every APIM user to assume exposure and rotate. We judge it likely that stolen secrets have already been used against services behind the gateway (KJ-3). Our confidence is Moderate. Six questions, short answers
Am I affected?
If you run APIM Classic on the Developer, Basic, Standard or Premium tier. Consumption and the v2 tiers are not. NSM asks every APIM user to treat stored secrets as exposed (page 2).
What was exposed?
Secrets in named values, client secrets for identity and OpenID Connect providers, subscription keys, and tokens for the direct management API (page 5).
Is there a patch or a CVE?
No CVE. The fix is server-side. Check that each gateway’s RuntimeVersion meets NSM’s threshold, and rotate anyway (KJ-2; B-6).
Would our logs show it?
Probably not in APIM. Look where the secrets were used: service-principal sign-ins, Key Vault, Storage, backends (KJ-5; page 8).
Who did it?
Unknown. The reported addresses are consumer ISP space in one Chinese province and a small hosting network; that locates proxies, not people (page 12).
Is it linked to the Entra Connect Health breach?
No evidence of a link. Different service, different data, same disclosure channel and the same fix date (page 11).

Executive summary: the gateway’s secrets were read, the door is now shut, and every copied key still works

Before 19 November 2026, an unauthorised party reached the management functions of Azure API Management (APIM) Classic instances and read the secrets they store. APIM is the gateway many organisations put in front of their APIs, and it holds the keys the gateway uses to reach the services behind it. Microsoft identified the flaw on 18 November, the day after Equinor disclosed an attack, and closed the path on 19 November. Norway’s National Security Authority (NSM) names Equinor and Skagerak Energi among the affected and says organisations in other sectors and countries were hit too.

The fix stopped new access. It revoked nothing already copied, and Microsoft says so. A stolen backend key or app secret works against the service that issued it, outside APIM, where the gateway’s rules and logs never see it. Indicators shared by Wiz point to stolen secrets used to sign in as applications and to reach Key Vault and Storage. We judge such use likely. Our confidence is moderate, because no victim has confirmed it and we have not seen Wiz’s data first-hand.

Ask for this weekAn inventory of every Classic instance, its RuntimeVersion checked, logs exported before 8 November ages out, and every stored secret rotated at its issuer, tenant access keys and app secrets first (A-1 to A-9).
Ask for within 30 daysSecrets held as Key Vault references or replaced by managed identities, and backends that accept APIM-held keys only from the gateway (A-10 to A-13).
Ask for within 90 daysA plan to leave the Classic tiers, or to put their management endpoints on a private network (A-17).

Not known: how many customers were read, by whom, from what date, and by which path. Microsoft has not said.

A Singapore analogy: the guardhouse key cabinet

A condominium’s guardhouse keeps a key cabinet: spare keys to every unit, the gym and the plant room, and the contractors’ passes. The guardhouse also has an old side window, from before the current counter, that the managing agent declared out of use but never boarded up. For some days, someone reached through and copied keys. On 19 November the window was boarded up. That stops further copying. It changes no lock. Every unit whose key hung in the cabinet must change its own lock, and the residents hold the screwdrivers, not the guards. The cabinet is the APIM secret store, the units are the services behind the gateway, and changing a lock is rotating a secret where it was issued. The side window is our judgement of the way in (KJ-4), not Microsoft’s.

Key judgements: two rest on Microsoft’s and NSM’s own words; four are inference and are graded lower

KJ-1

An unauthorised party used management functions of APIM Classic instances to read stored secrets, in more than one organisation and country.

Almost certain High

Microsoft’s advisory says customers were exposed and an unauthorised party could have used the flaw to reach stored secrets before Microsoft found it. NSM goes further: an unknown actor actively went after tokens, secrets and named values, and the affected include Equinor, Skagerak Energi and organisations in other sectors inside and outside Norway. Confidence is High because the provider and a national authority say so on record and agree.

Rests on: claims 1, 3, 8. Gap: no customer count, no first-access date and no per-tenant notice of access from Microsoft (Q-1 to Q-3).

KJ-2

Every secret an exposed instance held before 19 November stays usable until it is replaced at its issuer.

Almost certain High

Microsoft says its fix “does not automatically invalidate credentials that may have been accessed before the fix was deployed”. Its rotation guide adds that updating a value in APIM does not revoke the old credential at its issuer. APIM issues subscription keys itself; a backend key, a model key or an app secret is issued elsewhere, and only that issuer can revoke it.

Rests on: claims 5, 18. Gap: Microsoft has not said whether it can tell a customer that its own instance was read (Q-3). Until it does, NSM’s rule stands: treat every stored secret as exposed.

KJ-3

Stolen secrets have been used against services behind the gateway: to sign in as Entra applications and to reach Key Vault and Storage.

Likely Moderate

Wiz, as relayed by Anders Kristiansen, lists addresses used almost only for service-principal sign-ins with stolen secrets, and two more used with curl/7.88.1 against Key Vault, Storage and APIM gateways, in a window from 8 to 21 November. NSM says the actor actively sought secrets. A Reddit administrator described a valid admin token used against Key Vault; nobody has verified that account. Confidence is Moderate: the vendor is credible, but we read its data second-hand and no victim has confirmed downstream use.

Rests on: claims 8, 22, 23; E-01 to E-06. Gap: a victim or NSM confirming downstream use would raise this to almost certain; Wiz withdrawing the indicators would lower it to roughly even.

KJ-4

The way in was the legacy direct management API at <name>.management.azure-api.net.

Likely Low

Four facts line up. The affected tiers are exactly the tiers on which Microsoft offers that API (E-07). Microsoft’s exposed list names “tokens used to call direct management APIs”, and its rotation guide adds the tenant access keys that sign them. The API accepts a token signed with a key, not an Entra sign-in, so Conditional Access and RBAC never see it. And in 2024 Binary Security showed a Reader could take the admin keys that sign such tokens. Against it: Microsoft has named no path, and the claim that the API still answered with its switch set to No comes with no published test.

Rests on: claims 4, 6, 18 to 22, 25; E-07. Gap: a Microsoft root cause would settle it either way (Q-4).

KJ-5

The access itself left no record customers can rely on; evidence of compromise sits downstream, where the secrets were used.

Very likely Moderate

A flaw confined to APIM’s Classic tiers sits in APIM’s own endpoints, not in Azure Resource Manager, so we reason that it would not reach the Activity log; on KJ-4’s path, calls bypass Resource Manager by design. Microsoft has given customers no query and no per-instance record of access. NSM asks owners to map what sits behind each instance, not to search APIM. If the path did run through Resource Manager, hunt H-1 will show list-secret calls, so run it anyway.

Rests on: claims 19, 20; ref 15. Gap: a Microsoft statement that the path ran through Resource Manager would lower this to unlikely.

KJ-6

The actor swept APIM Classic instances broadly rather than aiming at Equinor; the energy companies were the first to notice.

Likely Moderate

Equinor said on 19 November that the attack did not appear to target it specifically. NSM reports victims in several sectors and outside Norway. The flaw sat in a shared service, and Microsoft fixed it for every customer at once. Against: two Norwegian energy companies hit within a day also fits deliberate targeting, and an actor can sweep broadly and then choose victims from what it took.

Rests on: claims 8, 12, 15. Gap: the list of affected sectors and countries (Q-1).

A stolen backend key skips the gateway, so the gateway’s limits and logs never see it

Key Vault references narrowed the exposure; they did not remove it

Microsoft ties exposure to instances that kept secrets in APIM itself rather than as Key Vault references (Reported, The Register). A reference keeps the value out of APIM’s store, but the gateway still fetches and uses it, and anyone who administers the instance can make it show: Kristiansen notes that Microsoft documents request tracing and policy editing as ways named values, Key Vault-backed ones included, can be read. NSM draws no line between the two. Rotate referenced secrets too, after the inline ones.

Model keys are named values too

Microsoft sells APIM as an AI gateway for Azure OpenAI and other model endpoints, and documents two ways to reach them: managed identity, or a provider API key kept as a named value and injected by policy. A copied model key bills inference to its owner and escapes the gateway’s token limits. Sandbu asserts that model access was the actor’s goal but cites no evidence; we do not assess the actor’s aim (page 12). Compare provider-side usage with gateway metrics (H-7).

The key is the identity. No user signs in, so MFA, Conditional Access, Privileged Identity Management and Azure roles never apply. Each token carries its own expiry; Microsoft’s documentation does not say whether regenerating the key cancels tokens already issued.

Microsoft’s exposed list fits this door. “Tokens used to call direct management APIs” are a secret only this door uses, and Microsoft’s rotation guide of 21 November adds the tenant access keys that sign them.

Researchers have walked this door before. In September 2024 Binary Security showed a Reader could fetch the built-in administrator’s keys (user ID 1) through Resource Manager and sign a token for this API. Microsoft closed that route; the door stayed.

Retired is not removed. Sandbu reports the endpoint still answered with “Enable API Management REST API” set to No, although Microsoft’s documentation says calls fail when it is off. He publishes no test, so the claim is Unconfirmed (claim 21).

From gateway to backend: the chain has five stages, Microsoft closed the first two, and the last three are the customer’s

Walking the chain

1 FindThe internetAssessed

Classic instances are named on one public pattern: the gateway at <name>.azure-api.net, the direct management API at <name>.management.azure-api.net, the portal at <name>.developer.azure-api.net. Unless an instance sits in an internal virtual network, these names resolve publicly and can be guessed or found in DNS. Microsoft ties exposure to instances not set up to its network guidance; Sandbu reads that as sparing instances whose endpoints were not public.

BREAK IT HERE Developer and Premium instances can take their endpoints off the internet in internal virtual-network mode. Basic and Standard Classic cannot, which is one reason to leave them (A-17).

2 EnterManagement planeConfirmed

An unauthorised party reached management functions that should have refused it. That is Microsoft’s statement; how is not (Q-4). The administrator on Reddit described a zero-day authentication bypass in a deprecated API that led to a privileged system account. Unverified, it matches KJ-4: every instance’s built-in administrator is user ID 1, and the direct API admits whoever can sign a token for it.

BREAK IT HERE Microsoft closed the path on 19 November. On the customer side, switch the direct API off and regenerate tenant access keys, so a copied key or token cannot reopen it if the endpoint still answers (A-6).

3 HarvestSecret storeConfirmed

NSM: the actor “actively” went after access tokens, secrets and named values. Microsoft lists what could be read (page 5). An instance administrator can also surface Key Vault-backed values, through request tracing or a policy that echoes them.

BREAK IT HERE Nothing recalls a copied secret. Replace each at its issuer, test, then revoke the old one (A-5). Updating the value in APIM alone leaves the old credential working.

4 UseEntra, Key Vault, StorageReported

Wiz’s indicators describe two kinds of use. Service-principal sign-ins with stolen secrets came almost only from consumer broadband ranges in Shandong, China, where an app that runs in Azure should never sign in. Key Vault, Storage and gateway calls came from two addresses on a small hosting network, with curl/7.88.1 as the client. Both are cheap to change (page 9).

BREAK IT HERE Backends that accept APIM-held keys only from the gateway’s addresses turn a stolen key into a refused call (A-12). Workload-identity Conditional Access can do the same for app sign-ins where licensed (A-13).

5 ExpandTenant, backendsAssessed

No source reports this stage. It is what the harvest allows: an app secret carries that app’s Graph and Azure permissions, and administrative access to an instance could leave users, subscriptions, identity providers or policies behind. Changes made through the direct API would appear in no Activity log, so only a comparison with source control finds them.

BREAK IT HERE Diff each instance’s configuration against source control and remove what nobody can explain (A-7). Cut each APIM-linked app’s permissions to what the gateway needs.

The pattern outlasts the addresses

Home-broadband addresses pass risk engines that score hosting networks as suspicious, which suits sign-ins; a hosting box suits bulk pulls with curl. That split is a choice, and choices recur. An app that runs in Azure has no reason to sign in from any residential address, in Shandong or anywhere else: alert on that, not on these 20 addresses.

What the indicators cannot tell you

They show where traffic left from, not who sent it. Consumer ranges in one province and a residential-proxy flag fit a rented proxy pool as well as an operator at home. No hashes, domains or tooling have been published. Attribution: page 12. Before blocking ranges this large at a backend, check whether staff, partners or customers legitimately use them.

Precedents: researchers have opened APIM’s management plane from the side before; this is the first reported misuse

Binary Security’s 2024 findings, reported by GBHackers, showed that a user with only the Reader role could reach the administrator’s keys or an administrator token for APIM’s management API. The 2025 developer-portal finding, which let an account from another tenant sign up where the owner thought sign-up was off, was closed as working by design (Reported). None of the three was reported as misused. The common thread is a second way into administration that sits beside the one owners manage.

Glossary: terms used in this report, in plain words

APIMAzure API Management: Microsoft’s service for publishing APIs behind one gateway that checks callers, applies rules and forwards calls to the services behind it.
Classic and v2 tiersThe two generations of APIM. Classic: Developer, Basic, Standard, Premium, and Consumption. v2: Basic v2, Standard v2, Premium v2.
GatewayThe part of APIM that receives API calls, at <name>.azure-api.net.
Management planeThe functions that configure an instance, as opposed to the gateway that carries traffic.
Azure Resource ManagerAzure’s standard management interface. Calls need an Entra sign-in and an Azure role, and land in the Activity log.
Direct management APIAn older, per-instance management interface at <name>.management.azure-api.net. It accepts a signed token instead of a sign-in. Retired in March 2025.
Tenant access keyThe instance key that signs tokens for the direct management API. Whoever holds it can act as the instance administrator.
SAS tokenShared access signature: a token made by signing a user ID and an expiry with a key.
Named valueA setting APIM stores and inserts into its rules, often a password or API key. Kept in APIM or as a Key Vault reference.
Key Vault referenceA named value that points to a secret in Azure Key Vault instead of holding it.
Subscription keyThe key a caller sends to the gateway to use an API.
Request tracingA debugging feature that records how the gateway handled a call, values included.
Service principalAn application’s identity in Entra ID. It signs in with a client secret, a certificate or a managed identity.
Client secretA password for an application. Whoever holds it can sign in as the application.
Managed identityAn identity Azure manages for a resource, with no secret for anyone to copy.
Identity provider, OpenID ConnectThe service that signs users in, and the protocol many use. APIM holds client secrets for those it trusts.
Developer portalAPIM’s website where outside developers read API documentation and get subscription keys.
RuntimeVersionThe build of APIM software a gateway runs. NSM uses it to check whether Microsoft’s fix has arrived.
Activity logAzure’s record of management operations made through Resource Manager.
Azure Service HealthThe page in the Azure portal where Microsoft posts incidents and security advisories to the customers affected.
Conditional Access for workload identitiesEntra rules that limit where and how an application may sign in. Needs a separate licence.
Residential proxyA service that routes traffic through home internet connections, so it looks like ordinary users.
CVE, MSRCA CVE is a public catalogue number for a vulnerability. MSRC is Microsoft’s Security Response Center, which issues them for Microsoft products.
NSM, PSTNorway’s National Security Authority, home of its national CERT; and its Police Security Service.

Appendix B: five hunting queries checked against Microsoft’s schemas, and two shell checks; none run on live data

B-1 to B-4 are KQL for Sentinel or Log Analytics; B-5 is for Azure Resource Graph. Each parses with Microsoft’s Kusto parser, and every column and function resolves against Microsoft’s table reference (E-08). None has been run on live data, so false-positive rates are untested. Expect hits from your own automation in B-1 and from legitimate users of the large ranges in B-2 to B-4; confirm each against behaviour (page 8). AzureDiagnostics workspaces need column names changed. B-6 and B-7, shell commands, were not run; B-7 follows Microsoft’s documented Tenant Access operations.

B-1 (H-1) Secret reads through Resource Manager
AzureActivity
| where TimeGenerated > datetime(2026-11-01)
| where ResourceProviderValue =~ "MICROSOFT.APIMANAGEMENT"
| extend Op = toupper(OperationNameValue)
| where Op endswith "/NAMEDVALUES/LISTVALUE/ACTION"
    or Op endswith "/LISTSECRETS/ACTION"
| summarize Events = count(), Ops = make_set(Op, 10),
    IPs = make_set(CallerIpAddress, 10),
    First = min(TimeGenerated)
    by Caller, _ResourceId
| order by First asc

B-4 (H-2) Gateway: new subscriptions, traces, ranges
let ranges = dynamic(["113.120.0.0/16","113.124.0.0/16",
    "123.168.0.0/16","120.221.0.0/16","222.173.70.0/24",
    "23.162.24.0/24"]);
let known = ApiManagementGatewayLogs
    | where TimeGenerated
        between (datetime(2026-10-01) .. datetime(2026-11-08))
    | where isnotempty(ApimSubscriptionId)
    | distinct _ResourceId, ApimSubscriptionId
    | extend Seen = true;
ApiManagementGatewayLogs
| where TimeGenerated > datetime(2026-11-08)
| join kind=leftouter known on _ResourceId, ApimSubscriptionId
| extend NewSub = isnotempty(ApimSubscriptionId) and isnull(Seen),
    FromRange = ipv4_is_in_any_range(CallerIpAddress, ranges)
| where NewSub or FromRange or IsTraceRequested == true
| summarize Calls = count(), Apis = make_set(ApiId, 10),
    IPs = make_set(CallerIpAddress, 10),
    Codes = make_set(ResponseCode, 5),
    Traced = countif(IsTraceRequested == true),
    First = min(TimeGenerated)
    by _ResourceId, ApimSubscriptionId, NewSub, FromRange
| order by First asc

Appendix C: Singapore, where to report and what to check

ReportSend confirmed or suspected use of a stolen APIM secret to SingCERT: singcert@csa.gov.sg, or CSA’s Cyber Aid tool at go.gov.sg/csa-cyber-aid. Include the credential type, the service it reached, source addresses and times in UTC. Report to the police if money or data was taken.
Check (A-16)Owners of critical information infrastructure and MAS-regulated institutions should check their incident-reporting terms and deadlines. If an API behind the gateway serves personal data and a stolen key reached it, assess whether the PDPA’s breach-notification duty applies. Not legal advice: your DPO or counsel decides.
